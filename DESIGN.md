# Design of *The River That Reverses*

*Recorded 2026-10-21. This file is a design record, not a poem and not a claim about the path. It is
stamped when it is pushed and is never edited afterward; a change is a new file or a numbered revision.*

## What the book is

Eighty-one poems that distill meditation, each traced to a practice or a citation. The book does for the
inner turning of the mind what the *Tao Te Ching* does for the outer Tao: it points with images at what
cannot be pointed at directly.

Life and work are covered by two other books in the library (*The Seventh of January* and *Two
Singularities*), so they are not the source here.

## The idea the book is built on

Taking nibbāna as object is what makes a path lokuttara (supramundane): the magga and phala cittas take it
as object, and so does the gotrabhū citta just before them, which is the turning itself. The stopping of
the stream is a consequence of what that turning removes, not what the turning aims at. The desire that
points the practice is chanda, a wholesome factor that the path drops at its end. It is not craving for
non-becoming.

## Structure: four parts, by the four tetrads

The four parts follow the four tetrads of mindfulness of breathing (MN 118): the body, feeling, the citta
and the dhammas.

This is **an arrangement of the breath practice, not a partition of the path.** The texts do not support a
stage ladder by object or by mode:

- Tetrads 1 to 3 are samatha and vipassanā together; only tetrad 4 is pure vipassanā (Visuddhimagga,
  `e0101n.mul.xml:6853`).
- Nibbāna already appears as object inside tetrad 4 (`e0101n.mul.xml:6841`, `:6849`).
- Other routes exist, such as dry insight without jhāna (`abh03a.att.xml:813`, `:833`).

Parinibbāna lies outside the four parts. It is a non-occurrence that no one performs. The parts are of
unequal length, and 81 is not made to fit four.

## Voice: a literary device, ours and not canonical

| Part | Tetrad | Voice |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | body | second person, *you*: the teacher's instructing voice |
| 2 | feeling | first person, *I* |
| 3 | citta | third person, *it* |
| 4 | dhammas | the impersonal, with no agent |

The suttas keep *I will train* in every tetrad (`s0203m.mul.xml:1805`–`:1829`), so this scheme belongs to the
book, not to the texts. The voice in part 2 is first person because the commentary reads that tetrad as
the most inward (`s0203a.att.xml:1593`–`:1605`).

Cautions kept:

- A *you* poem never reports a lack in the reader and never instructs by indictment.
- The impersonal voice in part 4 does not assert that no one exists as a realized view.
- Whose *I* speaks in part 2 is not yet ruled.

## Provenance

Every poem has an endnote in `sources.md` naming a practice or a citation. A poem with no source does not go
in. Parts 3 and 4 rest on citations. No poem claims an attainment.

## The image, and what it cannot do

The river is the Tonlé Sap, which reverses its flow each year. The Dhamma is called *paṭisotagāmin*, going
against the stream. Three limits:

1. A river entering the sea is also an image of a self dissolving into a greater self. The poems must not let a
   river-body persist, since a river's identity over time is only a convention.
2. The Tonlé Sap reverses and then returns every year. It can carry the turning and the emptying, but not the
   end. Part 4 therefore works by negation and silence, with the fewest poems and no extended image.
3. The lokuttara moment is a break, not a flow.

## Still open

- Whose *I* speaks in part 2.
- Whether the rule against indicting the reader applies to poems.
- Unchecked: the order in which Pāli grammar names the persons; what a "fourth person" is in Algonquian and
  Navajo (an obviative, not an impersonal); whether the *Tao Te Ching* avoids *I* (it probably does not).
- The mapping of the four parts to the seven purifications has not been tested.
